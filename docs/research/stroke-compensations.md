# Compensatory movements in stroke reaching, and what a wrist + sternum IMU pair can see

Scope: the project's hardware (2x MPU-6050, wrist + sternum, no magnetometer, pitch + roll only, threshold on peak trunk tilt from an upright zero). Every claim carries a bracketed reference to the list at the end. Items marked **UNVERIFIED** were not confirmed against the paper itself.

## Summary (bottom line first)

- **Trunk flexion (forward lean) is the dominant, best-documented compensation in stroke reaching, and the sternum IMU's pitch can detect it. Lateral lean (roll) is detectable too.** Trunk motion is the most-studied compensation in technology-based studies: 46 of 63 reviewed studies on "awkward use" assessed the trunk, mostly in the sagittal plane (41/46) [R1].
- **Rotation, shoulder elevation, scapular, shoulder abduction and elbow deficits cannot be measured reliably with this hardware.** Rotation is yaw (drifts without a magnetometer); the others need a sensor on the arm or shoulder girdle. Hip/pelvis shifts are only seen as a combined trunk lean.
- **No validated cutoff exists.** The literature does not give a standard "compensated if trunk flexion > X degrees". The reviews and trials found use baseline-relative criteria (person's own unconstrained reaches, or percent difference from controls) [R1, R9, R10]. The 10 degree start value is therefore a tuning default, not a literature value.
- **Healthy people lean too, but mainly when the target is beyond arm's length.** In one study of healthy adults, trunk flexion was about 0 degrees at arm's length and about 15-19 degrees when the target was 20 cm beyond [R11]. So 10 degrees is only a sensible cutoff if the target sits **inside** arm's length for every tester; place the target by measured arm length.
- **Prior IMU work is mostly small, on healthy people with simulated impairment.** About 90% accuracy with sternum + arm IMUs [R4]; a trunk + wrist pair gave macro-F1 0.80 in able-bodied people and reduced performance in 4 neurological participants (preprint) [R5]. Nothing found validates a simple tilt threshold on stroke patients. That fits the project's scope (team members simulating compensation), but say so in the report.

A pure lateral pelvis translation with no tilt would be missed. Mostly a limitation to state. |

## Thresholds in the literature

- **No standard cutoff found.** The 2022 systematic review of 72 technology-based compensation studies reported trunk angular displacement (12/41 sagittal studies) and linear displacement (8/41) as the common measures; it did not report degree or cm thresholds for defining compensation (in the portion I read; the page was truncated at about 100k of 175k characters) [R1].
- **Healthy baseline vs. target distance (the most useful numbers).** Five healthy adults reached forward with the target at arm's length vs 20 cm beyond arm's length. Trunk flexion (video motion capture, day 1): 0.4 degrees at arm's length, 18.7 degrees at +20 cm. Kinect v2 gave -0.4 and 15.0 degrees; Kinect v1 gave -2.2 and 10.3 degrees [R11]. The extended condition was designed to mimic stroke-like compensation [R11]. Implication for the project: at arm's length a 10 degree threshold sits well above healthy noise, but a target placed beyond reach would make healthy testers exceed 10 degrees. n=5, so treat as indicative.
- **Fraction of reach, not angle.** In forward reaching, trunk flexion accounted for 34.0% (SD 28.5) of the movement in stroke vs 3.0% (SD 2.8) in healthy controls [R6]. Useful as a sense of the gap, not as a cutoff.
- **Baseline-relative criteria.** A study of 31 sub-acute stroke participants scored compensation as percent difference from healthy-control means, mapped to a 0-10 scale, with a score of 1 or more counted as compensation; the authors call it exploratory and unvalidated [R12]. **UNVERIFIED:** a trial registry description says the Valdés feedback bands were set from each person's baseline reaches; I did not confirm this in the paper. Conclusion: personalised baselines are the norm; this supports the project's calibrated upright zero and suggests also recording each tester's own clean reps.
- **Feedback works.** Visual or force feedback reduced anterior trunk displacement by 45.6% and 41.1% respectively from baseline in 15 stroke survivors, with no difference between modalities [R10]. Supports the real-time cue concept, though it used a 3D camera, not an IMU.
- **Definition used for "lean forward" in a classifier paper:** hip flexion below 90 degrees only; no numbers given for the other classes, and the authors say an earlier spine-angle/shoulder-line threshold method performed poorly (no values given) [R9].
- **UNVERIFIED, left out of the above:** a VR study that reportedly triggered feedback at 90% of each user's mean trunk flexion (Foreman & Engsberg 2019; the paper returned HTTP 403 and I saw only a search summary; I did open its abstract on Europe PMC, which does not state the threshold) [R13]. A patent snippet mentioning 7% and 20% thresholds is unverified and not used.
- **Measurement error matters for the 10 degree decision.** Against video motion capture, Kinect v2 trunk flexion had bias of about 3.3 degrees and v1 about 8.4 degrees on the extended forward reach [R11]. The side-view phone video that the project uses as ground truth will have its own angle error; label by clear visual lean, not by a measured 10 degrees.

Take-away: a sternum IMU for lean plus a wrist IMU is a sensible minimal pair [R5], and lean forward is the easiest compensation to detect across modalities [R9]. Nothing found shows a plain tilt threshold validated in stroke patients.

## Do healthy people also lean?

- Yes, and it depends on how far the target is. In six healthy adults reaching at 60%, 100% and 140% of arm's length, trunk and upper-arm motion increased with distance while forearm motion decreased [R16].
- Healthy participants used little trunk for targets within reach; stroke participants used the trunk even for the closest targets. The authors propose the trunk joins in near the limit of arm extension, which is lower after stroke [R3].
- Fifteen healthy participants reaching beyond arm's length on stable and mobile seats showed substantial hip and trunk range of motion [R17] (abstract does not give degrees).
- Trunk use by healthy adults at +20 cm: roughly 15-19 degrees, see Thresholds [R11].
- Design consequence: fix the target at a measured fraction of arm length (inside it, for example 90%; the 90%-of-arm-length setting was used in a bimanual-reaching study [R14b]) so that "clean" healthy reps stay near zero tilt.

## Limits and open questions

- **Sensor can't see most compensations** (rotation, shoulder, scapular, elbow); the claim of the device must be "trunk lean during a forward reach", not "compensation" generally.
- **Sternum tilt vs trunk tilt.** One sensor on the sternum cannot separate hip flexion, spinal flexion or posture slouch. A chest strap shifts with breathing/skin; recalibrating upright zero each session is needed.
- **Pitch/roll accuracy** from a complementary filter on an MPU-6050 during dynamic motion is a project question; no literature number was gathered for it.
- **No patient validation.** Testers are team members. Healthy people simulating compensation may differ from real compensation (timing, magnitude, variability); in the Kinect study the classifiers failed on stroke data [R9], and the trunk-wrist IMU preprint degraded on neurological participants [R5].
- **Open:** (1) Is the target placed inside arm's length for every tester? (2) Tune the 10 degree cutoff on recorded data per tester vs one shared value? (3) Does wrist-IMU rep segmentation survive the arm slowing/segmenting seen in stroke reaching [R2]? (4) Did not find a verified paper giving a clinical cutoff for trunk flexion in degrees.
- **Not verified / not accessed:** PubMed and Springer/AHA/SAGE pages were blocked, so for [R2]-[R3], [R7]-[R8], [R14], [R16] I read abstracts via the Europe PMC API only, not full texts; I could not get trunk displacement values in cm for the Levin/Michaelsen papers. The scapular pilot, Foreman & Engsberg's threshold and the patent figures are UNVERIFIED.
